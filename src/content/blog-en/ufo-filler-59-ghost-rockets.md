In the spring of 1946, shortly after the end of World War II, Nordic countries began to see a surge in reports of mysterious flying objects. Swedish residents frequently observed high-speed "rocket-shaped" objects streaking across the sky, emitting green or white light, and some even reported hearing loud explosions. Nordic media dubbed these phenomena "Ghost Rockets" (Spökraketer).

From May to December 1946, over 2,000 sighting reports flooded into the Swedish Ministry of Defense. In July, the Swedish military established a special investigation committee called the "Swedish Defence Staff's Flying Phenomenon Investigation Group." This committee comprised top scientists and military experts, including members from the Swedish Aeronautical Research Institute.

Witness descriptions were remarkably consistent: the objects were elongated, rocket-shaped, about 2-5 meters long, flying at high speeds (estimated at over 1,000 km/h), with some exhibiting exhaust trails. Most perplexing was that Swedish radar stations repeatedly locked onto these objects, confirming they were not optical illusions.

Records from Finland were even more dramatic. In July 1946, a "ghost rocket" was reportedly seen crashing into a lake north of Helsinki. Finnish border guards conducted a search operation, claiming to have found a metal object about 2.5 meters long at the lake bottom—but this discovery was suddenly classified, and the Finnish government refused further comment.

Initially, the Swedish military suspected these were remnants of Soviet tests of Nazi V-2 rockets. However, after analysis, they found that the flight characteristics of the ghost rockets—such as sharp turns, hovering, and jerky acceleration—did not match any known rocket technology.

The U.S. Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) also closely monitored the phenomenon. In August 1946, the U.S. Embassy in Stockholm sent a classified dispatch to Washington, reporting that the Swedish military was "extremely concerned" about the ghost rockets. The U.S. Air Force's Project Blue Book later used the ghost rocket files as foundational data for early UFO research.

The ghost rocket phenomenon gradually subsided by the end of 1946, but a large number of official documents still state that it remains "unexplained." This phenomenon is considered a precursor to the modern UFO wave—predating the Kenneth Arnold incident (1947).
